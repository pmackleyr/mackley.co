I've fought to breathe since 1998: asthma, childhood pneumonia, a speech impediment, collegiate boxing, jiu jitsu, now yoga. Each chapter pulled me one breath deeper.

Mackley is my middle name. I've gone by Parker most of my life—the name my parents know, the name most people know. Parker listens too much to others, cares too much about what they think, lives second-hand.

Mackley is who I am inside, who I know I can be. The name comes from my great-grandfather Sunny, a light for my mom and her family. Mackley steps up, he listens deeply, acts with courage, and lives first-hand.

Everyone has a Mackley within them—the person you become when you breathe deeper and commit to what's calling. Our purpose is to breathe life into that person.

From this purpose, the goal wasn't to invent anything new. It was to find what matters most and start the deepest breathe possible.

The journey began in the U.S., moved through Europe, and led into India in search of the roots of yoga. In one old ashram, older yogis rinsed their noses in the sink each morning at 4 a.m. No explanation. It was simply the first thing they did every day.

That observation is where MACKLEY begins.

You enter the world on an inhale. You leave when breathing stops. Breath is the most critical function in your life—yet it's largely ignored and forgotten.

Breath comes before thought. Your brain doesn't work without it. Breath comes before movement. Your muscles can't contract without it. Breath comes before emotion. Your nervous system is regulated by breathing rate, depth, and rhythm.

The quality of your breath determines the quality of your life.

You breathe over 20,000 times a day—more than anything else you can consciously do. Breath sits upstream of your heart rate, blood chemistry, sleep quality, wakefulness, reaction time, and recovery. It runs quietly in the background while you work, rest, and sleep. It changes with posture, stress, training, air quality, and whether air enters through your nose or your mouth.

Air is best through the nose. The nasal passage filters particles, warms and humidifies air, and provides the resistance needed to fully inflate the lungs. Mouth breathing bypasses all of this. Over time, breathing becomes shallow, weaker, and less oxygen reaches the brain and body.

Most people think this is normal. They call it "congestion," "allergies," or "stuffiness." Because it happens slowly, you don't notice. Shallow breathing becomes your baseline. Mental fog becomes ordinary.

The contrast is obvious once you can finally take a deep breath. Air reaches deeper into the lungs. You feel fuller. More alive. You wake up.

The truth: your breathing capacity isn't fixed. It's automatic—but it's also changeable.

In yoga, a kriya is a simple, repeatable action with a predictable physical result—a habit. You already do some every day, like brushing and flossing. The very first kriya in yoga is clear breath, because everything else depends on whether you can mechanically breathe.
