The best tool for the most important habit.

You enter this world on a breath. You leave when breathing stops. The shape of your life is determined by the shape of every breath you take in between. We can go much longer without food or water than without breath.

Jala neti is one of yoga's cleansing kriyas, or habits. I think of it as “clear breath.” It's no coincidence that the yogis I met at the Bihar School of Yoga, an ashram in Bihar, India, started each morning clearing their breath with a pure copper neti pot, salt, and lukewarm water.

It's a subtle practice. You may not notice a change right away. For me, it's a way to take in more life and see a little more clearly with all three eyes.

How it works:

  1. Measure your nasal saline mix according to the package directions.
  2. Fill the pot with the specified amount of lukewarm distilled or sterile water, or tap water you've boiled and cooled, and mix. Never use water straight from the tap.
  3. Rinse each nostril.
  4. Wash the pot with mild soap and safe water, then dry it.

Lean over the sink, tilt your head sideways, and breathe through your mouth. Place the spout in the upper nostril and pour gently, letting the rinse flow through your nasal passage and out the other nostril. Use the rest of the potful to repeat on the other side.

At first your nose may sting a little, but afterward it may feel clearer. Life is full of pollution, dust, and debris. A lukewarm saline rinse can help flush that noise from your nose, along with mucus. Even if you don't think you'll benefit from jala neti, give it a try: your first breath may just surprise you.

Questions

What is jala neti?

Jala neti is the yogic practice of rinsing your nose with salt water. A neti pot uses gravity to wash away dust, pollen and mucus.

How do I use it?

Fill the clean pot with lukewarm saline. Lean over the sink, tilt your head sideways, and breathe through your mouth. Put the spout into the upper nostril and pour gently so the rinse flows out the lower one. Repeat on the other side. Ask a health professional how often to rinse for your needs.

What water and salt should I use?

Use distilled or sterile water, or tap water you've boiled and cooled. Never use water straight from the tap. Use a measured nasal saline mix and follow its directions for how much water to use. Aim for a comfortable rinse. If it stings, check the mix and temperature rather than adding salt by feel. Read the CDC guidance.

Is nasal rinsing right for everyone?

Do not rinse if your nose is fully blocked. Ask a health professional first if your immune system is weak, or before using it with a child. Stop and get medical advice if you have pain, nosebleeds, fever or headaches.

Why copper?

I prefer pure copper to plastic or ceramic. Copper has a long association with healing in yogic tradition, and it's what the older yogis I met in Bihar used. Copper does not make tap water safe for nasal rinsing.

How do I clean and dry it?

After each use, wash the pot with mild soap and safe water. Dry the outside with the included microfiber towel, then set the pot upside down until the inside is completely dry. Copper can darken naturally over time.

A little lemon juice can brighten the outside, but it isn't needed for cleaning. Keep it out of the pot, rinse it off promptly, and dry with the towel.
